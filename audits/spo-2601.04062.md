# Audit — Wang & Hasuike, *Smart Predict–then–Optimize Paradigm for Portfolio Optimization in Real Markets* (arXiv:2601.04062v3)

导读（中文）：这是 portfolio-lab 的北极星靶子。本文档是**只读审计** —— 全部结论从论文自身的表格和设计推出，没有用任何新数据、没有装任何新依赖。核心结论有三条：(1) 忠实复现不可能，实验设定有 6 处关键信息缺失；(2)「consistently improves」被论文自己的表格证伪 —— 21 次 DFL-vs-PtO 比较里只赢 8 次；(3) 就算他们宣称的差距是真的，他们的设计也只有 13% 的概率能检出它。

| | |
|---|---|
| Predictor | Linear, in every setting (deliberate: interpretability / Occam) |
| Decision layer | `ŵ = argmax_{w∈W} r̂ᵀw` — a **max-return LP**, eq. (2) |
| Variants | + transaction fee `γ‖w−w_{t−1}‖₁` (5); + `ℓ₂` penalty `λ‖w‖₂²` (6) |

| Data | daily US ETFs, 2015-01-01 → 2025-01-01 |

| Reported window | 2016–2024 |

## 2. Reproducibility: a faithful replication is not possible

Six pieces of information required to rerun the experiment are absent from the paper:

1. **The asset universe.** Neither the number of ETFs nor a single ticker is stated
   anywhere. §4.1 says only "U.S. exchange-traded funds." This alone blocks replication.
2. **The feasible set `W`.** Referenced in every formula, defined nowhere. Long-only?
   Fully invested? Box caps? Leverage? Under a max-return LP this choice *is* the
   strategy — see §3.
3. **Feature parameters.** SMA over what window; RSI period; MACD spans; Bollinger
   lookback and width; which volume indicator. All unspecified.
4. **The Optuna configuration.** Search space is given for `lr` and epochs (Tables 2–3)
   but not the objective, the number of trials, or the pruning rule.
5. **`λ = 0.42`.** A three-significant-figure value with no stated provenance. If it was
   tuned on backtest performance, it is a free parameter selected on the test set.
6. **No code, no data statement.**

**Consequence for us.** Any number we produce will differ from theirs for reasons we
cannot attribute. So this cannot be a replication in the sense that Phase 1 was. It has
to be an **audit + re-test under a proper null**. That reframing is not a concession —
given §3 and §4, the audit is the stronger contribution.

## 3. The comparison the paper makes is not the comparison it claims

**Their "PtO Markowitz" baseline contains no covariance matrix.** Eq. (17) reads

```
w_PtO = argmax_{w∈W} r̂ᵀw
```

That is a max-return LP, identical in form to the SPO decision layer of eq. (2). There
is no `Σ`, no risk-aversion parameter, no mean–variance trade-off. Calling it "the
classical Markowitz model" (§3.3.2) is incorrect, and it makes the baseline sound more
principled than it is.

What the paper actually compares is therefore narrower than advertised:

> the *same* degenerate LP decision layer, with a linear predictor trained by MSE
> versus the same linear predictor trained by SPO+.

**Why the layer is degenerate.** `argmax r̂ᵀw` over a simplex puts 100% of capital in
the single asset with the highest predicted return — a corner solution that changes
completely whenever the argmax changes. This is exactly the pathology that makes a
naive two-stage baseline look terrible, and it is the same failure mode we already
documented in Costa & Iyengar, where their `po_net` turned over 58% of the portfolio
per week and went Sharpe-negative at 25 bps (`notes.md` iteration 2). The `ℓ₂` variant
(6) turns the LP into a QP and would diversify — but it is offered as an *SPO* variant,
not applied to the baseline.

**Turnover is never reported.** With a 50 bps fee charged on `‖w_t − w_{t−1}‖₁` and
corner-solution portfolios, turnover is the dominant driver of every net number in the
paper. It appears in no table. Our iteration-2 finding says this is where the whole
comparison lives.

**1/N is absent.** The DeMiguel et al. (2009) null appears nowhere, nor does any
buy-and-hold benchmark. Given that the best reported strategy earns 14.05% at 19.09%
volatility over 2016–2024, a passive equity benchmark is the first thing a reader needs
and the paper does not supply it. *(How a passive benchmark actually compares must be
measured, not asserted — that is the point of §6 Route B.)*

## 3b. Two specification defects found while implementing it

Both surfaced only by writing the method out; both are recorded in
`polab/spo.py` and pinned by tests.

### The printed SPO+ loss (eq. 4) is not the SPO+ loss

The paper gives

```
L_SPO+(r̂, r) = max_{w∈W} (2r̂ − r)ᵀw − rᵀw⋆
```

The correct max-form surrogate (Elmachtoub & Grigas 2022, translated from their
minimization convention) is

```
L_SPO+(r̂, r) = g(2r̂ − r) − 2r̂ᵀw⋆ + rᵀw⋆ − h(w⋆)
```

The printed version drops `−2r̂ᵀw⋆` and carries the wrong sign on `rᵀw⋆`. Both
expressions equal 0 at `r̂ = r`, which is why the error is easy to miss, but they
differ by `2(r̂ − r)ᵀw⋆` — a term **that depends on `r̂`**. The printed loss
therefore has subgradient `2w̃` where SPO+ has `2(w̃ − w⋆)`: a different
algorithm, not a different formatting of the same one.

They use PyEPO, which implements SPO+ correctly, so this is almost certainly
typesetting. It matters anyway: the paper is the only specification available,
and anyone implementing from it alone does not get SPO+.

*(Separately, the paper asserts in §3.1.3 that gradients still flow when the
`ℓ₂` term is added, but never shows the SPO+ **bound** survives a non-zero
prediction-independent penalty `h`. It does — the derivation is in
`polab/spo.py` and checked numerically by `test_spo_plus_upper_bounds_regret`.)*

### `λ = 0.42` makes the ℓ₂ layer collapse to 1/N — or not, depending on units the paper never states

Measured on our actual daily ETF panel, sweeping the regularization weight:

| λ | mean max weight | ‖w − 1/N‖₁ | effective N (of 9) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0.42** | 0.125 | 0.056 | **8.94** |
| 0.1 | 0.173 | 0.247 | 8.10 |
| 0.01 | 0.508 | 1.181 | 3.02 |
| 1e-3 | 0.901 | 1.697 | 1.24 |
| 1e-5 | 0.999 | 1.776 | 1.00 |

At decimal-return scale the penalty at `w = 1/N` is `0.42/9 ≈ 0.047`, roughly
**five times** the typical daily `|r| ≈ 0.009`. The return term is then
irrelevant and the layer simply minimizes `‖w‖²` — i.e. returns equal weight
whatever the predictor says. **Their "SPO+ with ℓ₂" variant is, at this scale,
1/N wearing a decision-focused label**, which would explain both its
middle-of-the-pack Sharpe (0.550) and the paper's description of it as "more
conservative and diversified."

The qualifier matters: if their `r̂` is in percent, the same `λ` concentrates
instead. The paper states neither the units nor the scaling, so `λ = 0.42` is
uninterpretable, and the behaviour it induces swings from *equal weight* to
*corner solution* across the plausible range. Pinned by
`test_l2_layer_collapses_to_equal_weight_at_the_papers_lambda`.

## 4. "Consistently improves" is falsified by the paper's own tables

The abstract and conclusion both claim decision-focused training **consistently**
improves over PtO. Counting every DFL variant against the PtO Markowitz baseline in the
paper's own Sharpe columns:

**Table 4 — full window 2016–2024** (PtO = 0.659)

| DFL variant | Sharpe | vs PtO |
|---|---|---|
| Softmax-MaxReturn | 0.362 | −0.297 ✗ |
| Softmax-MaxSharpe | 0.166 | −0.493 ✗ |
| RobustSPO (ρ=0.01) | 0.688 | +0.029 ✓ |
| RobustSPO (ρ=0.1) | 0.595 | −0.064 ✗ |
| SPO+ (γ=0.005) | 0.715 | +0.056 ✓ |
| SPO+ (γ=0.005, λ=0.42) | 0.550 | −0.109 ✗ |
| SPO+ | 0.785 | +0.126 ✓ |

**3 of 7 win.** Against the *classical* MaxSharpe baseline (0.574): 4 of 7.

**Table 5 — COVID 2020** (PtO = 0.796): 4 of 7 win. The classical MaxSharpe baseline
(1.179) beats 4 of the 7 DFL variants.

**Table 6 — 2024 bull market** (PtO = 1.699): **1 of 7 wins**. Classical MaxSharpe
(1.793) beats 6 of 7. The paper concedes this and attributes it to PtO's "strong
exposure to bullish market conditions."

**Aggregate: 8 wins out of 21 DFL-vs-PtO comparisons — 38%.** A method that loses 62%
of its own reported head-to-heads is not "consistently" improving anything. The only
variant with a defensible claim is plain `SPO+`, which wins 3/3 — and it is also the
variant with by far the highest volatility (19.09 vs ~14–15 for everything else),
i.e. its Sharpe edge is bought with concentration under a corner-solution LP that
charges no fee in its own objective while still being charged 50 bps in evaluation.

### An anomaly in Table 5

`RobustSPO (ρ=0.1)` and `SPO+ (γ=0.005)` report **identical values on all five metrics**:

```
46.92 | 18.61 | 2.170 | 2.322 | −9.58
```

The text describes them as producing "almost overlapping NAV paths." Identical to three
decimals on five distinct metrics is not "almost overlapping." These are different
models with different objectives, and they differ in Table 4 (0.595 vs 0.715) and in
Table 6 (0.903 vs 0.745). Two rows coinciding in exactly one of three tables is more
consistent with a table-construction error or a run-labelling bug than with a modelling
insight. **This should be raised with the authors before it is cited.**

## 5. The design cannot detect the effect it reports

This is the decisive point, and it needs no data — only the sample size.

Using `polab/sharpe_test.py`, the standard error of a **paired** annualized Sharpe
difference, as a function of sample length and the correlation between the two
strategies' return series:

| | ρ=0.99 | ρ=0.95 | ρ=0.90 | ρ=0.80 | ρ=0.60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| daily, 2016–2024 (T≈2264) | 0.047 | 0.106 | 0.149 | 0.211 | 0.299 |
| daily, 2020 only (T≈252) | 0.141 | 0.316 | 0.447 | 0.633 | 0.895 |
| monthly, 2016–2024 (T=108) | 0.048 | 0.106 | 0.151 | 0.213 | 0.301 |
| monthly, 2020 only (T=12) | 0.143 | 0.319 | 0.452 | 0.639 | 0.904 |

Their reported gaps, for comparison:

| comparison | gap |
|---|---|
| SPO+ vs PtO | **+0.126** |
| SPO+ (γ) vs PtO | +0.056 |
| RobustSPO (ρ=0.01) vs PtO | +0.029 |
| SPO+ vs MaxSharpe | +0.211 |

SPO+ runs at 19.09% volatility against PtO's 14.76% — these are visibly different
portfolios, so ρ ≈ 0.8–0.9 is the realistic column, giving a standard error of
**0.15–0.21 against a claimed gap of 0.126**. The headline effect is under one standard
error of its own estimator.

Monte-Carlo confirmation with the actual test (daily, T=2264, ρ≈0.95 — i.e. the
*generous* case, fat-tailed t(4) returns, gap set to a **genuinely real** 0.126):

```
power at 5% = 0.13   (38/300 detections)
```

**Even if their effect is real, their design finds it 13% of the time.** At the more
realistic ρ≈0.8 it is lower still. And every regime-specific claim is far worse: the
COVID-2020 and 2024 bull-market tables rest on one year each, where the standard error
is 0.3–0.9 and Sharpe ratios of 2.170 carry no information whatsoever.

Add to this that hyperparameters are re-searched by Optuna at each of ~108 rebalances,
with no correction anywhere for the resulting selection.

## 6. What we should actually do

**Route A — the audit above. Done, zero dependencies, zero new data.** §§2–5 stand on
the paper alone. This is already a result: the central claim is unsupported by the
paper's own numbers, and the design lacks the power to support it in principle.

**Route B — supply the missing null.** Rerun a defensible ETF universe through our
harness with 1/N and buy-and-hold present, at their 50 bps, and report turnover per
strategy. This does not reproduce their numbers (§2 makes that impossible) and should
not pretend to. It answers the question their table cannot: *does anything here beat
doing nothing?* Requires a data dependency — see the open decision below.

**Route C — implement SPO+ and test it honestly.** PyEPO is their implementation; SPO+
on a max-return LP is ~50 lines against `scipy.optimize.linprog`, no new dependency
needed, and our walk-forward engine already enforces causality mechanically. Judge it
with `sharpe_test.py` against 1/N. Given §5, a **pre-registered** statement of the
detectable effect size should be written *before* running it.

**Not worth doing:** chasing their exact numbers. Without the universe, `W`, and the
feature parameters, any mismatch is uninterpretable.

The ETF universe and the data source. Their universe is unknowable; ours must be chosen
and stated. Options and the dependency question are in the session hand-off — note that
`yfinance` / `pandas_datareader` is a **new dependency** (hard rule: ask first) and that
